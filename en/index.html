Text style (new in 0.35)

The out-of-the-square shape works well for titles and short lines, but in long passages the uneven bottom line tires the eye. Final consonants under horizontal vowels (곡, 굴, 원) sank to the depth of Latin descenders and looked like subscripts. Version 0.35 adds the Text style to Naemkung Typewriter. It keeps the same strokes and consonant shapes, but re-lays out each syllable for long reading.

  • When a syllable has a final consonant, the initial and vowel move up and the final grows, so bottom lines are far more even (semi-square)
  • Narrower Hangul cells and larger initials, so the gap between letters differs clearly from the gap between words
  • Smooth outlines with no wobble, and less stroke contrast
  • Adds the book punctuation “ ” ‘ ’ … — 「 」 『 』, and narrows the space after periods and commas

About the typeface

Korean three-set (Kong Byung-woo style) manual typewriters of the 1950s–70s struck the initial consonant, vowel and final consonant as separate pieces of type at fixed heights. As a result the top line of every syllable is even, while the bottom line rises and falls with the vowel and final consonant. Naemkung Typewriter follows this "out-of-the-square" (탈네모꼴) shape and the marks that type leaves on paper.

No other font files or outlines were used. The path of every stroke is written in code and then given weight. The build code is published in the repository together with the fonts.

  • All 11,172 modern Hangul syllables, 51 Hangul jamo, Latin letters, digits and symbols (ASCII)
  • Monospaced: every Latin cell is the same width, and every Hangul cell is the same width (in the Text style, only period, comma, ellipsis and dash differ)
  • Dotted zero; 1, I and l are drawn differently so they are easy to tell apart

In memory of Dr. Kong Byung-woo

Don't force Hangul into a square box — type it as it is, and type it fast.

The uneven bottom line of Naemkung Typewriter comes from the three-set typewriter of Dr. Kong Byung-woo (1907–1995). Dr. Kong was an ophthalmologist who opened Korea's first specialist eye clinic. Yet the work he devoted his life to was opening the way for Hangul to be written by machine, quickly and correctly.

His three-set typewriter typed the initial, medial and final parts separately, so Hangul was printed as it is naturally shaped instead of being squeezed into a square frame. People mocked its uneven look as a "clothesline font", but that very speed and accuracy became the foundation of Hangul mechanization. The doctor who healed eyes built a Braille typewriter for blind people, and at nearly eighty he cultivated three-set Hangul computing on the Macintosh.

When he died, he asked that his death not be announced and that no funeral be held. He donated his usable organs and his body, and left the rest of his estate to help blind people. This typeface is a small thank-you letter to that spirit.
